# Design Principles

These principles guide discovery and later design. They are intended to constrain solutions without prematurely choosing technologies.

## 1. Capture once, organize many ways

A piece of project knowledge should not need to be duplicated merely because it participates in several useful views.

## 2. Relationships matter as much as containers

Folders, pages, and hierarchies are useful presentation mechanisms, but they should not be the only means by which knowledge is organized. Important relationships between concepts, decisions, evidence, work, and artifacts must be representable directly.

## 3. Preserve reasoning, not only outcomes

The project record should retain enough context to explain how important outcomes were reached, including alternatives, uncertainty, failed approaches, and superseded conclusions.

## 4. Current truth and historical truth are both valuable

Updating current knowledge should not require erasing prior knowledge. The system should make it possible to determine both what is authoritative now and what was believed or decided previously.

## 5. Provenance should be first-class

Important assertions and decisions should be traceable to their sources, evidence, observations, discussions, experiments, or other origins whenever practical.

## 6. Epistemic state should be visible

A question, hypothesis, observation, interpretation, decision, and verified result are not equivalent kinds of knowledge. The system should avoid presenting them as if they carried the same status.

## 7. Capture must tolerate incompleteness

Useful knowledge is often born messy. The system should allow low-friction capture first and progressive enrichment later rather than requiring perfect classification before information can be recorded.

## 8. The canonical model should be separable from its projections

A chronological journal, architecture view, decision record, tutorial, and subsystem page may present overlapping information differently. Presentation should not silently become the only source of truth.

## 9. Humans and tools should share the same project memory

Machine-queryable structure should not require maintaining a parallel body of knowledge disconnected from the material humans actually use.

## 10. Prefer inspectable and portable representations

The project should resist unnecessary dependence on opaque storage or proprietary semantics. Project knowledge should remain inspectable, exportable, and recoverable.

## 11. Avoid ontology for ontology's sake

Structure exists to reduce cognitive burden and improve traceability. Every modeled distinction should earn its complexity through a real use case.

## 12. Validate against real engineering work

The model should be tested against actual project information, including ambiguous, contradictory, incomplete, and superseded material. Synthetic examples are insufficient.

## 13. Dogfood continuously

Project Knowledge should use its own emerging practices and tooling to preserve its development. Friction encountered while doing so is product evidence.

## 14. Keep implementation hypotheses reversible

During inception and discovery, proposals such as knowledge graphs, temporal databases, wikis, event sourcing, or specific schemas remain hypotheses until requirements justify them.

## 15. Optimize for recovery of orientation

A central measure of usefulness is whether a person can efficiently recover where the project is, why it is there, and what matters next.
